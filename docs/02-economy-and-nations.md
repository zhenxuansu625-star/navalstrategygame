# Economy, production and nations

The economy works like HOI4: civilian factories build things, dockyards build ships. The central choice is short term versus long term. You can convert effort into dockyards and ships now, or grow a bigger industrial base that out-produces rivals ten years later.

## Buildings

| Building | What it does | Built by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Civilian factory | Builds every other building (10 factories max per construction project); some are taken by consumer goods | Civilian factories |
| Naval dockyard | Produces ships and refits through production lines | Civilian factories |
| Naval base (levels 1–10) | Where fleets repair and resupply; level sets repair speed and capacity | Civilian factories |
| Infrastructure | Speeds construction in a state; more building slots | Civilian factories |
| Refinery / synthetic plant | Fuel for fleets | Civilian factories |

## The short vs long term trade-off

- Each civilian factory spent on construction can build either **more civilian factories** (compounding growth) or **dockyards** (ships sooner).
- Building civilian factories first is slow at first, but it pays off over 5–10 years. Rushing dockyards gives an early fleet that rivals may later out-build.
- **Consumer goods** take a share of civilian factories, and this share rises with war economy laws. Going to a war economy early boosts dockyards, but it eats into civilian growth.
- **Economy laws** (Civilian economy → Partial mobilisation → War economy → Total mobilisation) trade consumer goods and construction speed against production. Higher laws need high tension or war.

**States and slots:** each state has a fixed number of building slots set by its size and infrastructure. Coastal states can hold dockyards and naval bases. Running out of slots forces hard choices or expansion.

**Resources:** ships need steel, and later designs need chromium and oil. Shortfalls slow production lines. Resources come from your states or from trade, and trade costs civilian factories.

**Naval production lines:** each line produces one ship design with a number of dockyards assigned. More dockyards make it faster, up to a cap. Production efficiency grows the longer a line runs, so switching designs often is costly. Lines can be ordered by priority.

## Historical nations

The player picks one of the historical naval powers; all others are AI. Each starts with its historical fleet, industry, doctrine leaning and geography.

| Nation | Starting strengths | Starting weaknesses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| United Kingdom | Largest fleet and dockyards, global naval bases | Fleet spread thin across an empire |
| Germany | Strong civilian industry, fast-growing tech | Small fleet, bottled up in the North Sea |
| United States | Huge industrial potential | Small navy and peacetime economy at start |
| France | Solid fleet, Mediterranean and Atlantic bases | Divided between two seas |
| Japan | Focused Pacific navy, strong torpedo tradition | Few resources, needs imports |
| Italy | Central Mediterranean position | Weak industry and fuel shortages |
| Russia / USSR | Large potential | Three separated fleets, weak dockyards |
| Austria-Hungary | Compact Adriatic fleet | Bottled up behind the Otranto Strait; dissolves if it loses a major war |

Starting values are game-balance numbers, tuned for play rather than taken from historical statistics. **Historical events** (e.g. the Anglo-German naval race, the Washington Naval Treaty) fire around their real dates, but the player's choices can change the outcome.

**Tension and treaties:** tension is tracked per rival (0–100). It rises with arms races, incidents and colonial disputes, and unlocks higher economy laws and war. Naval treaties can cap tonnage and gun size; breaking them raises tension with every signatory.

**Events** are short choices with trade-offs, e.g. "Parliament demands cheaper cruisers: gain 2 civilian factories but cap cruiser size for 5 years."
